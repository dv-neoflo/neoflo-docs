AI in Finance:
Where Are We?

A summary of market research on AI adoption in the finance function

Fifteen studies  ·  June 2024 – March 2026

Finance is one of the functions that has enormous potential to be transformed through AI. It is extremely quantitative, relies on complex rules, suffers from huge documentation, and is always talent constrained. To examine how AI is getting adopted in finance, we examined industry research published between June 2024 and March 2026. Estimates of AI adoption in the corporate finance function show an extremely wide divergence, ranging from 11 to 93 percent. The variation is not primarily a disagreement about practice. It reflects differences in what each study defined as adoption, and those definitions are rarely stated alongside the headline figure. In this white paper we assimilate the findings of these reports to establish how finance is adopting AI, where the ROI is being generated, and what the open questions are.

Section 01

Introduction

In 2024 the finance function at Algar Telecom, a Brazilian telecommunications company, put an AI agent into production for a single task. Since most billing errors in telecoms surface on the first invoice issued to a new customer, the agent compares the signed contract against the record entered in the CRM, and both against the invoice that was sent. Over the first nine months of 2025 it reviewed approximately a quarter of all first invoices. The company’s chief financial officer has attributed US$1.5 million in additional profit to the work.

The example is instructive less for its scale than for its structure. The task is high-volume, rule-bound and has a determinate right answer; the scope is a defined share of a single process; and the outcome is stated as a figure with an attributable source. Those four properties — bounded task, verifiable output, defined scope, quantified result — recur throughout this review as the conditions under which finance AI deployments have been shown to work.

Most of the available anecdotal evidence is less specific. Walmart’s corporate controller has described running scenario models daily where the team previously ran them monthly. The chief financial officer of Hewlett Packard Enterprise has described deploying agentic tools across the finance organization to reduce routine processing. A finance technology leader at Johnson & Johnson has characterized the direction of the profession as a convergence of the accounting profession with the engineering profession. These accounts are plausible and consistent with the survey data reviewed below, but they are not independently verifiable, and they appear in reports produced by firms active in the finance transformation market. They are included here as context rather than as evidence.

Beneath the case material, two years of survey research have produced a reasonably consistent account of the field. Finance was a comparatively late adopter and has largely closed the gap: in 2023, human resources, legal and procurement were roughly twice as likely to be using or scaling AI, and Gartner reported that difference substantially narrowed a year later. Most finance functions now report using AI in some form, a figure commonly cited at around 60 percent. However, demonstrated value has not kept pace with deployment. The reported obstacles are operational rather than technical — data quality, legacy systems, skills and security. And agentic systems, which execute rather than recommend, are widely identified as the next phase, with planning, working capital and profitability management named as the likely applications.

In this whitepaper, we examine fifteen studies conducted between June 2024 and March 2026 by various firms examining how AI has been adopted in the finance function across organizations. These studies and their sample sizes are presented in Figure 1. Most of the larger studies apply revenue floors: US$1 billion for Deloitte and EY, US$250 million globally and US$500 million in the United States for KPMG. Studies that include smaller companies generally survey respondents outside the finance function. The resulting coverage of the mid-market finance function is mapped in Section 10.

The published research cannot tell you whether your finance team is behind, because the available benchmarks are measuring different things. The 93 percent figure comes from a survey counting heavy or light AI use anywhere in a company. The 11 percent figure comes from a survey counting production use inside the finance team alone. Each is accurate for what it asked. Neither works as a benchmark for one department.

Nonetheless, two conclusions can be drawn with reasonable confidence. First, approximately one in five organizations can demonstrate measurable value from AI in finance — a figure that two independent surveys arrive at by different routes, discussed in Section 6. Second, for organizations in the US$100 million to US$1 billion revenue band, nearly every published benchmark was measured on substantially larger companies, and often on respondents outside the finance function.

Section 02

Scope and methodology

The purpose of this review is to establish what has already been examined in the published research on AI in the corporate finance function: which questions have been asked, of which populations, by which organizations, and with what methodological documentation.

There is a practical reason for doing this. Fifteen studies already exist, so the field is not short of research. What is missing is any attempt to square them with one another. Without that, numbers measuring different things get compared as if they were the same, and a finance team trying to work out where it stands can reach very different conclusions depending on which report it happens to read. Working out where the evidence agrees, where it does not and why, and what it never covered has to come before anyone commissions another survey.

This document is therefore a literature review and contains no original survey data. It covers material published between June 2024 and March 2026.

We would like to clarify that by the finance function we mean the internal finance and accounting organization of an operating company: accounts payable, receivables and collections, the general ledger, reconciliation and the close, FP&A, controls and reporting.

Scope exclusion: financial services

Research on AI in banking, insurance, capital markets, lending and wealth management is out of scope. That is a different subject — AI built into financial products and services — and the two get confused constantly because both file under “AI in finance.” Algorithmic trading, credit underwriting, automated advice, claims processing and fraud detection are excluded here, however good the research.

To be included, a study had to report quantitative findings on adoption, value, barriers or use cases; had to be publicly available at least in summary, a requirement that introduces a bias noted in the methodology; and had to be in English.

How figures are graded

Numbers in this field are usually picked up second-hand — an article quoting a press release about a report nobody can read. Detail is lost at every step: sample sizes get swapped, percentages get measured against the wrong base, and separate survey waves get merged into one. So every figure here was traced back toward whoever published it and graded by what we could confirm.

Primary
Confirmed in something the publishing organization released itself — a press release, report PDF or methodology page. Sample size and question wording checked where disclosed.
Secondary
Only available through a third party reporting on the study. Usable directionally, not safe to quote as a precise figure.
Contested
The study, its methodology or its reported sample is subject to substantive public dispute.

Where a frequently circulated version of a figure differs from the publisher’s own reporting, this review uses the publisher’s figure and identifies the difference at the relevant point in the text.

Section 03

Findings and their implications

Six conclusions supported by source-verified data.

Most of the disagreement is about definitions, not practice. Fifteen studies report figures between 11 and 93 percent. Group them by what each one counted instead of by the size of the number, and the spread inside each group falls to about fourteen points: studies counting production use land between 11 and 25 percent, and studies counting any use at all land between 56 and 93. The 82-point gap measures the questions, not the companies. Primary

Growth has flattened, and use is still shallow. Gartner is the only firm to have asked the same question three years running: 37 percent in 2023, 58 in 2024, 59 in 2025. One jump, then a plateau. RSM, surveying mid-market companies, found 91 percent using generative AI somewhere in the business but only a quarter of those running it inside core operations. AI has become common without becoming deep. Primary

Far more companies have deployed AI than can show what it returned. In Deloitte’s spring 2025 survey of 1,326 finance leaders at companies above US$1 billion in revenue, 63 percent had fully deployed AI and were using it, 21 percent could point to clear and measurable value, and 14 percent had also put agents into production. That leaves about two-thirds of deployments without a documented result at the time of the survey. Primary

Deloitte and KPMG only look like they disagree. Deloitte reports 21 percent delivering measurable value; KPMG reports 71 percent saying AI is meeting or beating expectations for return. The two asked different questions. KPMG also published a stricter version of its own — return beating expectations — at 23 percent, within two points of Deloitte. The gap comes from whether the question asks about expectations or about evidence. Primary

The obstacles change as teams get further along. Deloitte split its barrier question by maturity, and the order flips. Teams early on are held up by old systems, cited by 41 percent, and unclear return, cited by 30. Among teams that have delivered measurable value and put agents into production, those drop to 31 and 21 percent — while worry about data security climbs from 44 to 57. Getting AI working trades one set of problems for another rather than clearing them. Primary

No study asks what a system should do when it is unsure. All fifteen treat accuracy as a bar to clear. None asks finance leaders to choose between coverage and reliability — say, between a system that codes every invoice at 94 percent accuracy and one that codes 85 percent of them at 99.5 percent and hands back the rest marked uncertain. Every real deployment settles this question. No published research says how.

Section 04

Adoption and depth

Published estimates of how many finance teams use AI vary enormously. Almost all of that variation comes from four choices each study makes and rarely reports next to its headline number. Who is counted: whether AI use is credited to the finance team or to the company as a whole. What counts as use: running in production, used anywhere, or merely being evaluated. Who was asked: the revenue floor for taking part, and whether respondents work in finance at all. And when: in a field moving this fast, two otherwise similar surveys can be two years apart.

Gartner has asked a consistent question three years running, which makes it the only usable time series here. It shows one step change and then a plateau.

Depth is a different story from headline adoption, and Deloitte has the cleanest measurement of it — a nested funnel, where each stage is a subset of the one before.

Other surveys find the same shape. RSM put 91 percent of mid-market organizations somewhere on the generative AI curve but only a quarter of those users deep inside core operations. Oliver Wyman and the New York Stock Exchange, surveying 494 finance chiefs, found between 70 and 81 percent still planning or piloting on the key finance use cases. Battery Ventures, working with 129 finance leaders at companies from $50 million to $5 billion, found 17 percent in production against 34 percent actively piloting — two pilots for every deployment.

Section 05

Use cases in production

Before asking whether AI in finance pays, it is worth establishing what it is doing. The distribution turns out to explain a good deal of what follows. Deployment concentrates in high-volume, rule-bound work: process automation, anomaly detection, analytics and operational assistance.

Set that against where finance leaders say agents would help most. The two lists barely overlap.

Production sits in process automation and anomaly detection. Ambition sits in planning, profitability and working capital. What separates the two lists is how much judgement the work requires: the deployed use cases have verifiable right answers, and the aspirational ones do not.

This is the most consistent structural pattern across the whole literature. AI earns its place fastest where the work is high-volume, rule-bound and checkable, and stalls where it must exercise judgement before the data quality and governance are there to support it.

It also sets up the value question. If most production deployment sits in work with verifiable answers, value should be relatively easy to demonstrate. The following section shows that it isn’t.

Section 06

Value realization

Since most deployments sit in work where the right answer can be checked, value ought to be easy to prove. The data say otherwise.

Deloitte reports 21 percent of its sample delivering clear, measurable value. KPMG reports 71 percent indicating that AI is meeting or exceeding expectations for return. Read together the two appear inconsistent. They are not: the questions measure different constructs, and KPMG’s own reporting contains the figure that reconciles them.

Reconciling the two measures

“Met expectations” and “delivered measurable value” are not the same test. Someone can report that AI met expectations because expectations were low, or because the question invites a satisfaction answer rather than an evidence-based one. Applying KPMG’s stricter filter — return beating expectations — gives 23 percent, within two points of Deloitte’s 21 percent. Two independent surveys, run by different firms using different questions, land on roughly one in five.

KPMG also supplies a mechanism. Organizations that formally track AI-related KPIs report materially better outcomes than those that don’t, and organizations with stronger governance and controls report up to three to six times the rate of significant improvement. The same study finds 33% of organizations increasing human-in-the-loop oversight in direct response to AI-related concerns, which is a rare piece of quantification for a design decision most vendors describe as a temporary stage.

Section 07

Barriers, by maturity stage

Barrier research usually produces a single ranked list, which suggests every company faces the same obstacles and differs only in how far along it is. Deloitte split the question by maturity instead, and got something more useful: the obstacles themselves change.

The direction of travel is the real finding. Old systems and unclear return — the two problems vendors and advisers spend most of their effort on — matter less as teams mature. Data security does not. It climbs from 44 to 57 percent and becomes the top concern among exactly those teams whose deployments are working.

One reading is that the early obstacles are obstacles to building, and money and effort clear them. What comes next are obstacles to trusting, and those grow heavier as the work being automated matters more. A system posting to the general ledger carries a different kind of risk from one drafting an internal report.

Battery Ventures surveyed comparable ground independently and ranks the entry barriers in a similar order, alongside two further figures worth noting.

4%
of Battery’s respondents said more than half their AI pilots succeeded — while 95% said they would pay a premium for AI capability that worked, and 71% named model inaccuracy as their leading concern.

Read together, these three figures describe a market with high stated willingness to pay, low demonstrated pilot success, and model inaccuracy identified as the principal concern. On this evidence the binding constraint on adoption is demonstrated performance rather than budget or intent.

Section 08

Skills and preparedness

Eighty-eight percent of the 1,446 senior finance and accounting people AICPA and CIMA surveyed last autumn expect AI to be the most transformative trend in their profession over the next year or two. Eight percent feel their organization is very well prepared for it, with another 21 percent feeling well prepared. Fifty-six percent name generative AI as their most prominent skills gap. It is the widest gap between belief and readiness anywhere in this literature.

The barrier ranking from the same study is worth setting beside Deloitte’s, because it draws on a different population — practitioners and managers rather than chief financial officers — and it puts people first. Half cite a shortage of human capital, skills and talent as the leading challenge, ahead of safety and security concerns at 47 percent and doubts about the technology’s maturity at 42.

Two readings fit. Either finance teams are under-skilled and need training, or a function whose job is to produce defensible, auditable numbers is applying exactly the standard that job demands, and the technology has not met it yet. Both are probably true. Raising adoption means training finance professionals in AI and building better ways to measure whether the tools actually work.

Section 09

The mid-market evidence

The largest and most rigorous studies set revenue floors that exclude most of the mid-market. Deloitte’s Finance Trends requires above US$1B. EY’s DNA of the CFO requires US$1B. KPMG requires US$250M globally and US$500M in the United States. What exists below those floors is smaller, less finance-specific, or both.

The best mid-market evidence comes from Grant Thornton, which finds companies in the $100M–$1B band reporting a structural advantage they aren’t converting. Only 29 percent of them name workforce skill gaps as a primary obstacle, against 46 percent of companies in the $1.1B–$5B band. On this measure the mid-market is better placed than its larger peers, not worse.

Grant Thornton’s reading is that mid-market firms have fewer layers of decision-making, a shorter path from strategy to execution, and leaders in closer contact with frontline teams — and still struggle to turn AI activity into measurable return. The constraint sits in governance and operational control, not in appetite or skills.

RSM’s data points the same way from another angle. Ninety-one percent of the mid-market companies it surveyed were using generative AI; a quarter had it fully integrated; 92 percent reported problems during rollout; 53 percent described themselves as only somewhat prepared to implement it. The caveat is that RSM surveyed technology decision-makers across all functions rather than finance leaders, so the findings describe the company rather than the department.

The highlighted cell is the US mid-market finance function: companies between US$100 million and US$1 billion in revenue, measured on finance operations specifically. These companies face much the same compliance obligations as far larger ones, but run finance teams too small to build their own systems — which changes the economics of automation completely. No study in this review looks at them directly.

The other three gaps are about what was asked rather than who was asked.

  1. The abstention trade-off

    Every study treats accuracy as a threshold to clear. None asks respondents to trade coverage against reliability: would you deploy a system that codes 100% of invoices at 94% accuracy, where six in a hundred are wrong and nothing flags them, or one that codes 85% at 99.5% and routes the rest to your team marked uncertain? That decision gets made in every production finance AI deployment, and there is no published data on how finance leaders resolve it.

  2. The confidence threshold for unsupervised action

    Related but not the same: what accuracy would a system need to demonstrate before a controller lets it post to the general ledger without review? Every vendor implicitly assumes an answer. Nobody has asked for one. And it almost certainly varies by workflow — the number for variance commentary won’t be the number for journal entries.

  3. The exception economy

    The literature reports touchless rates and straight-through processing percentages. It doesn’t report what happens to the residual: who resolves exceptions, how long they take, what they cost, whether anyone tracks it at all. KPMG’s finding that 33% of organizations are increasing human-in-the-loop oversight is the closest anyone gets, and it measures a policy response rather than an operational load. Human review time comes straight out of AI savings, so an unmeasured exception load is an unmeasured ROI.

These three questions are related. The rate at which a system declines to answer determines exception volume; exception volume determines the fully loaded cost of the deployment; and the confidence threshold determines the scope of work that can operate without review. The literature documents adoption extensively and does not address the parameters that determine whether a deployment is economically viable.

Section 11

Conclusion

Between them, the fifteen studies give a reasonably consistent picture of how AI is being adopted in finance, and say very little about whether it pays. Once definitional differences are accounted for, four findings hold up across separate surveys: adoption is now common but shallow, with production use concentrated in a minority of functions; deployment exceeds demonstrated value by a wide margin, with roughly one in five organizations able to evidence a return; value accrues fastest in high-volume processes with verifiable outputs and slowest in judgement-intensive ones; and the constraints on progress shift from implementation to assurance as organizations mature, with data security displacing legacy technology and unclear return as the dominant concern. These conclusions are unlikely to be materially revised by further surveys asking the same questions of the same populations.

The open questions are narrower and matter more. No published study measures what a system should do when it is unsure, how accurate it must be before it can run a given workflow unsupervised, or how many exceptions a live deployment throws off and what they cost to clear. These are not side issues: the exception load is what makes automation cost what it actually costs, and without it no return estimate can be checked against reality. Nor does any study in this set survey the finance function of a mid-market company directly, which leaves organizations in the US$100 million to US$1 billion band without a comparable reference point despite facing compliance obligations like those of much larger enterprises.

Closing those gaps would not take a large sample or a complicated survey. A survey of finance leaders at mid-market companies, fielded on the finance function specifically, could establish exception rates and resolution costs by process; measure the accuracy threshold at which respondents would permit posting to the general ledger without review, by workflow rather than in aggregate; and put the coverage-reliability trade-off to respondents directly, as a forced choice between a system that processes all transactions at moderate accuracy and one that processes fewer at high accuracy and flags the remainder. We hope to follow on the interesting work done in this area and answer some of these questions.

AI presents a massive opportunity in finance, and its impact can be like how AI is affecting software programming. Instead of finance being stuck in the mundane tasks of the back office, it can finally have a significant impact on the future direction of companies, and we are excited and cheering for this transformation.

Appendix B

Methodology

Selection. Studies published between June 2024 and March 2026 reporting quantitative findings on AI adoption, value realization or barriers within the corporate finance function, or within the mid-market broadly enough to bear on it. Studies of AI in financial services as an industry were excluded — a different subject that gets confused with this one constantly.

Verification. Each figure was traced back toward something the publishing organization released itself. Where we got there, the figure is graded Primary and the sample size, fielding window and population were checked against it. Where only third-party reporting was available, the figure is graded Secondary and isn’t used as a precise point estimate.

Limitations. Four are material. Several full reports are behind paywalls or registration requirements, so question wording could not be inspected in every case, Gartner in particular. Nearly all studies reviewed are sponsored by organizations active in the market being measured; sponsors are identified individually rather than adjusted for statistically. Sample construction is undisclosed for three studies. No study in this set was fielded specifically on US mid-market finance functions, which is the basis for Section 10. This review is itself produced by a company active in this market, and its selection and framing decisions should be assessed on the same terms it applies to the studies it examines.

Omissions. Where a source chart contained figures we couldn’t attribute unambiguously to a category, we left them out rather than estimating. That applies to two of the five barrier categories in Deloitte’s segmented data. Nothing here is interpolated, read off a chart, or reconstructed from a secondary description of a primary figure.

Frequently cited variants. Several figures in this field are commonly reproduced in forms that differ materially from the publisher’s own reporting. These include the combination of two separate Gartner waves and the denominator applied to the Deloitte funnel, both addressed in Section 4, and the apparent divergence between Deloitte and KPMG, addressed in Section 6. In each case this review uses the publisher’s own reporting.
